# 9. Social media: strategy

## 9.1 What is built

A **shareable summary card** on the public site: a picture of the national weekly numbers with the week, source and a sample-data notice, made on the server so no third-party tracking code loads. A **public attention** indicator uses Wikipedia page views per disease, shown to signed-in staff only. Nothing posts automatically.

## 9.2 Strategy

**Audience.** The public and journalists wanting a trustworthy weekly picture, and district teams, community leaders and partners who pass it on. Reach has limits: in 2025 Uganda had about 14.2 million internet users (28%) and 38.6 million mobile connections, but only about 2.4 million social media user identities (4.7% of people) [14]. Radio, SMS and messaging groups matter at least as much, so the card is built to be saved as one image and forwarded.

**Content.** Short, dated, sourced: last full week's national numbers, what changed, the ministry's own advice, and a link to the data page. No district or facility detail, no counts below five, no patient photographs, no advice that did not come from the ministry.

**Metrics.** Whether the message worked, not whether it was popular: reach, saves and forwards, clicks to the data page, the share of questions the page already answers, and the time from a false claim appearing to a correction. No individual tracking or advertising identifiers.

**Privacy.** Only national aggregates are posted, from the same source as the public page, so a post cannot show more. Sign-in data, cases and alerts are never published, and a named person posts, not an automatic job.

**Moderation.** A named communications officer owns each account, with a written comment policy (no personal health details, abuse or unverified claims). Comments with personal information are hidden at once and the person moved to private message. Questions get an answer within one working day; anything needing clinical judgement goes to the district officer; rumours that could cause harm go to the ministry.

**Misinformation.** In a large Twitter study, false news spread farther and faster than true news, driven by people rather than bots [15]. WHO treats the response as its own discipline: listen to community concerns, explain risk and expert advice, build resistance to false claims, and engage communities to act [16]. In practice: keep a rumour log; correct quickly, lead with the true fact and link the source rather than repeating the myth; publish likely questions ("what are the signs, where do I go") before a peak so the first answer people find is a good one; use trusted messengers such as district health officers; and never show sample data as real, which is why each public figure carries a notice while sample data are loaded.

## 9.3 Social media as a data source

Search and page-view data can show rising interest before reports arrive, but they are noisy and change with the platform: Google Flu Trends repeatedly overestimated flu as search behaviour and Google's own system changed [18]. So the attention indicator appears beside reported counts as context and never triggers an alert.
